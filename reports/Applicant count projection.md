# Shorten the clock, widen the channel

The square-root shape in odds' projection is about right. The parameters around it are not. The 28-day "posting life" mostly measures LinkedIn's automatic expiry, not how long employers keep reviewing. The displayed count also leaves out every applicant who came through another channel. Postings collect applications in a steep early burst followed by a long trickle. On the only large posting-level dataset, **45% of applications arrive within 48 hours and 60% within 96 hours**, which fits a power law F(t) = (t/L)^a with **a ≈ 0.4–0.5 over an effective life L of about two weeks**, not 28 days. LinkedIn's badge counts different things depending on apply type: completed submissions for Easy Apply, Apply-button clicks for off-site jobs. Either way it shows only LinkedIn's share of the pool, which is probably a third to a half of all applicants. In practice the two errors in the current model partly cancel. It projects too much growth for postings read early and too little pool for postings read late, and it treats every late applicant as competing with the whole eventual pile, which is wrong when screening is rolling. The recommended model replaces count × √(28/t) with a channel multiplier (≈1.9× off-site, ≈2.8× Easy Apply), a power-law time factor with L ≈ 14 days (or the real deadline), and a rolling-review weight that counts later arrivals only partly. Every NL-specific parameter is still an assumption. The cheapest way to change that is to calibrate on odds' own repeated reads and on ATS publication dates.

## Applications arrive in a two-day burst and then a long trickle

The best evidence on how applications build up comes from Davis and Samaniego de la Parra's Dice.com data: **125 million applications to 5.6 million US tech postings, 2012–2017**. **45.3% of applications go to postings less than 48 hours old and 59.9% to postings less than 96 hours old.** The daily rate per posting peaks at about 2.4 on day two and then "drops sharply to 1.0 per day and even fewer" ([NBER w32320](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32320/w32320.pdf)). Ashby's ATS data (about 13–14 million applications at US startups and scale-ups, 2021–2024) has the same shape over a longer window: week one runs **2–3× each later week**, and weeks two to four settle into a "steady state" ([Ashby 2023 Trends Report](https://www.ashbyhq.com/talent-trends-report/reports/2023-trends-report-applications-per-job)). Neither source publishes a fitted curve or a half-life, so the parameters below are back-of-envelope fits to the published points.

Those points rule out a single exponential decay. An exponential fitted to 45% at 48 hours implies a two-day half-life and 99% of applications by day 14, which leaves no room for Dice's ongoing trickle or Ashby's flat weeks two to four. A power law fits well. Fitting F(t) = (t/L)^a through the two primary Dice points gives **a ≈ 0.40**. Adding the secondary claim that 75% arrive within seven days ([arXiv 2305.02587](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.02587)) gives L ≈ 14 days and predicts F(4 days) = 0.60, which matches the reported 59.9%. Ashby's ratio of week one to the weeks-two-to-four average, applied to four-week postings, implies a ≈ 0.5–0.65. So the shape the app uses (a = 0.5) is defensible. The exponent is not the main source of error. The main source is L.

The exponent also varies with demand. In Dice's job-title quintiles, the most popular titles collect **49.8% within 48 hours against 32.9% for the least popular** ([NBER w32320](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32320/w32320.pdf)). That implies exponents of roughly 0.36 for high-volume titles and 0.55 for low-volume ones. Brand-name internships at ASML, Philips or ING probably behave like the top quintile: heavy alert traffic early, so a square root over-projects them when read early. Specialist engineering and sales roles are slower, with only 24–28% of applications in the first 48 hours. Employer size has no clear effect in either dataset. **No source anywhere gives an accumulation curve for internships, entry-level roles, remote roles, Easy Apply versus external apply, or any European market**, so US tech data is the prior until odds measures its own.

One behavioural note matters for an app that shows counts. In a LinkedIn field experiment with 2.3 million users, showing the applicant count raised application completion by **3.5%** ([Gee, IZA DP 10372](https://docs.iza.org/dp10372.pdf)). The badge is part of the system it measures, but the effect is too small to change the projection.

## The 28-day life is LinkedIn's timer, not the employer's

odds took its 28-day posting life as the median of 506 closed postings dated from LinkedIn and Indeed. Aggregator data show what that median measures. Across postings that closed in mid-2026, **LinkedIn and Indeed both have a median life of 30 days because closure is detected at the platform's 30-day expiry, which "reflects that expiry rule rather than employer behavior."** The same roles on the employer's own ATS close much sooner: **median 8 days on Lever and 14 days on Greenhouse and Ashby**, with 75th percentiles of 10, 36 and 33 days ([JobsPipe](https://jobspipe.dev/job-posting-lifespan.md)). Dice postings have a median of 7.0 days and a mean of 9.4, and **only 26% stay active beyond two weeks** ([NBER w32320](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32320/w32320.pdf)). LinkedIn's own engineers describe an average lifespan of "~3 weeks" ([LinkSAGE](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.13430v1)). LinkedIn's platform rules explain the clustering at 30 days: un-promoted posts close automatically after 30 days, and **free posts pause after 14 days or after a cap of about 10–30 applications** ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a517777)). JobsPipe gives no sample sizes and no seniority or country breakdown, so a 14-day effective life is a strong prior but not an NL internship measurement.

The posting date itself is also unreliable. SAP SuccessFactors jobs, which many large Dutch employers use, expire on LinkedIn at 30 days and must be reposted under a new URL ([SAP KB 2787433](https://userapps.support.sap.com/sap/support/knowledge/en/2787433)). **8% of postings reappear after a gap of ten or more days** ([JobsPipe](https://jobspipe.dev/job-posting-lifespan)), and recruiters report that LinkedIn reposts keep the old applicant tally ([Team Blind](https://www.teamblind.com/post/linkedin-applicant-count-ny1hws10); [Hiration](https://www.hiration.com/blog/over-100-applicants/)). A repost therefore shows a large count next to a small age, which is the worst case for a t^(−a) multiplier. The app's recent change to carry a count forward as a floor is the right defence. The age needs the same treatment: use the earliest first-seen date, or the ATS `first_published` timestamp where one exists.

Employers keep selecting long after the listing comes down. Ashby reports time to fill of **about 8 weeks for business roles and 10 weeks for technical roles** ([HR Dive](https://www.hrdive.com/news/recruiters-see-job-applications-triple-to-more-than-300-per-role/820096/)). Dutch time-to-fill benchmarks sit around **42–45 days** ([recruitmenttraining.pro](https://recruitmenttraining.pro/nieuws/time-to-fill/)). Seekers who hear back typically do so **6 days after applying** ([Huntr Q2 2026](https://huntr.co/research/job-search-trends-q2-2026)). The picture is a short intake window feeding a longer selection process.

## Whom a late applicant competes with depends on rolling or batch review

The current model treats the competitor set as everyone who will ever apply. That is right only for batch review, where the employer reads the whole pile after a closing date. Dutch universities and public bodies work this way. Radboud, for example, commits to answering "within four weeks after the vacancy closes" ([Radboud](https://www.ru.nl/werken-bij/sollicitatieprocedure)). Short-window entry-level postings on Magnet.me, such as one posted 24 July with a 1 August deadline, have a known end ([Magnet.me](https://magnet.me/nl-NL/vacature/1062219/startende-marktonderzoeker)). Private-sector postings with no closing date behave like rolling review. **20% of Greenhouse and Ashby postings and 44% of Lever postings close within seven days** ([JobsPipe](https://jobspipe.dev/job-posting-lifespan.md)), and recruiter accounts describe shortlisting from the first batch and stopping once interview slots fill ([Medium](https://medium.com/@unblit/is-high-application-volume-quietly-killing-your-hiring-speed-776dd340e5b7)). Even windowed corporate traineeships are not cleanly batch: Unilever's Dutch Future Leaders Programme reportedly fills places first-come, first-served within a three-week window ([Unilever careers](https://careers.unilever.com/NL-UFLP)). That detail comes from search snippets and is unverified for 2026.

Under rolling review, a candidate applying on day t competes fully with the C(t) applicants already in, and only partly with those who come later, because some of them arrive after the shortlist has formed. The popular "apply in the first 96 hours for 8× the interview odds" figure rests on about 1,600 applications and an unpublished method ([paulvanderlaken.com summary of TalentWorks](https://paulvanderlaken.com/2017/10/24/talent-works-data-science-to-improve-job-application-chances/)). No audit study varies application timing, so the size of the late-arrival discount is a judgement call. The direction is not in doubt. For a reader on day 20 of a rolling LinkedIn posting, the main risk is not a large pool. It is that the employer has already stopped reading. Greenhouse survival data put only **35% of postings still open at 14 days and 27% at 30** ([JobsPipe](https://jobspipe.dev/job-posting-lifespan.md)). JobsPipe's own survival and median figures for Ashby and Lever disagree, so treat these as rough. This risk belongs in odds as a separate "still reviewing" probability, not as extra applicants.

## LinkedIn's badge counts one channel, and sometimes only clicks

LinkedIn has published no definition of its public "N applicants" badge. Its advertising documentation separates "Job Apply Clicks" from "Job Applications" and says that when applications are collected on another site, "you'll only be able to review reporting on Job Apply Clicks" ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/lms/answer/119095)). Recruiters report that the counter goes up as soon as Apply is clicked, before the external page loads ([Resunova](https://www.resunova.io/blog/linkedin-applicant-count-clicks-not-applications/); [Daily Dot](https://www.dailydot.com/news/linkedin-lying-about-number-of-applicants/)). The badge is therefore two different quantities. On an **Easy Apply** job it approximates completed LinkedIn submissions, a floor for that one channel. On an **off-site apply** job it counts people who clicked through, an upper bound on LinkedIn-sourced applications. The display stops at **"Over 100 applicants"**, and some scrapers report "Over 200", so a role at 101 looks the same as one at 4,000 ([Hiration](https://www.hiration.com/blog/over-100-applicants/); [Apify northbell](https://apify.com/northbell/linkedin-jobs-applicants-scraper)). Above the cap the badge is only a lower bound. Indeed counts only completed on-Indeed applications and can show zero for redirect jobs ([Augusta support](https://support.augusta.care/hc/en-us/articles/19713153791501-Indeed-Why-Does-My-Job-Show-0-Applicants)).

To turn the badge into a total pool, divide by LinkedIn's share of applications, s, and for off-site jobs multiply by click-to-completion, c. Neither has a published NL value. Job boards as a group supply **49–61% of applications** in US vendor data ([CareerPlug via StaffingHub](https://staffinghub.com/hiring/company-pages-referrals-result-in-more-hires-recruiting-metrics-report/); [Fountain](https://www.fountain.com/posts/why-you-need-to-invest-in-your-career-site)). Dutch job seekers name Indeed as their favourite board slightly more often than LinkedIn, **45% versus 36%** ([Werf&](https://www.werf-en.nl/data-donderdag-indeed-favoriete-jobboard/)), but more of them use LinkedIn, **61% versus 49%** ([recruitmenttraining.pro](https://recruitmenttraining.pro/nieuws/vacaturesites-in-nederland/)). For internships, the university is still a large channel: **36% of European students found their last internship through it** in 2018 ([CEU on JobTeaser/EDHEC](https://www.ceu.es/blog/en/2018/study-shows-youngsters-trends-when-starting-their-first-job/)). A working value is s ≈ 0.35 (range 0.25–0.5), higher for English-language multinational roles and as low as 0.15–0.25 for Dutch-language or hourly roles where Indeed dominates. The only evidence on click completion is one hiring manager's estimate that about 20% of clickers never submit ([Resunova](https://www.resunova.io/blog/linkedin-applicant-count-clicks-not-applications/)). Long corporate ATS forms lose more applicants ([Joveo on Appcast](https://www.joveo.com/blog/what-is-apply-rate/)), so c ≈ 0.65 (0.5–0.9) suits Workday or SuccessFactors employers. Appcast's 5–6% "apply rate" measures an earlier step, from ad click to application, and does not apply here. These assumptions give **≈ ×2.8 (×2–4) for Easy Apply counts and ≈ ×1.9 (×1.0–3.0) for off-site counts.**

## Pools cluster near 60–140, and interviews near 3–5% of applicants

No Dutch source publishes applicants per internship or graduate posting. The best distributional data are British. ISE's 2024/25 survey of large employers reports a **mean of 101 and a median of 63 applications per internship vacancy, and a mean of 123 and a median of 82 per placement** ([ISE](https://ise.org.uk/knowledge/insights/508/5_internship_and_placement_trends_to_watch_in_2026/)). It also reports **140 per graduate vacancy**, ranging by sector from 74 (charity and public) through 188 (finance) and 205 (digital/IT) to 290 (retail and FMCG) ([Times Higher Education](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/733909); [Wonkhe](https://wonkhe.com/blogs/graduate-jobs-and-recruitment-reality/)). A mean-to-median ratio of about 1.6 implies a log-normal spread of σ ≈ 0.9–1.0. A Dutch student guide's figure of "about 50 responses per internship" is unsourced but consistent ([Boekhuis extract](https://download.boekhuis.nl/9789403619873_fragm-docb.pdf)). Ashby's figure of 300+ per role covers all seniorities, mostly US tech with remote roles, and should not be used as an NL prior ([HR Dive](https://www.hrdive.com/news/recruiters-see-job-applications-triple-to-more-than-300-per-role/820096/)). Pools are growing fast. ISE matched-employer internship applications rose 34% in a year, and applications per hire have tripled since 2021 in Ashby's data, so any prior needs a date stamp.

Conversion data say how many of those applicants get a conversation. **About 3% of applicants reach interview** at US small businesses ([CareerPlug](https://www.careerplug.com/recruiting-metrics-and-kpis/)). The figure is **4.7% for business roles and 3.6% for technical roles** in aggregated vendor data ([candidate.fyi](https://candidate.fyi/post/recruiting-funnel-benchmarks)). About **27% of interviewees are hired**, which implies roughly four interviewed candidates per hire. Gem counts 20 interview *events* per hire in 2024, spread across several rounds ([Gem](https://vercel.gem.com/blog/10-takeaways-from-the-2025-recruiting-benchmarks-report)). Ashby says EMEA candidates interviewed per hire reached "double digits" ([Ashby EMEA release](https://pr.columbiacountyspotlight.com/article/New-Data-From-Ashby-Reveals-EMEA-Recruiters-are-Absorbing-Rising-Application-Volume-Without-Slowing-Hiring/6aa12efa06b0f49a92996fcf)). ISE's 101 applications per internship hire implies about 1% from application to hire. Against this evidence, **odds' 10 slots for internships and 8 for other roles fall at the high end**. They are defensible only if "interview" includes recruiter phone screens. Counting hiring-manager interviews only, a single opening supports about 4–6 slots. For international students, Dutch field experiments find non-western-background applicants **about 40% less likely to be contacted** ([UvA](https://www.uva.nl/content/nieuws/persberichten/2019/07/gediscrimineerde-sollicitant-kan-weinig-doen-om-baankans-te-vergroten.html)). No study tests English-CV non-EU graduates directly.

## A cheap, lawful calibration loop exists, and scraping is the last step

No public source in the Netherlands shows an applicant count that can be read repeatedly except LinkedIn's logged-out job pages. Apify actors package those pages with history tracking, flags for closed and vanished postings, and flags for capped counts ([Apify northbell](https://apify.com/northbell/linkedin-jobs-applicants-scraper)). A full panel would enrol about 70 new NL postings a day across 6–10 role buckets. Each posting would be read every six hours for its first 72 hours, then daily, for about 25 reads. That is roughly 52,500 fetches a month, costing **about $30/month** ([Apify prodiger](https://apify.com/prodiger/linkedin-jobs-scraper)) **to $63–121/month** on the northbell actor. A 300-posting pilot costs about $4–17. Greenhouse's job-board API is public, unauthenticated, and returns `first_published`, `application_deadline` and implicit closure, but no applicant counts ([Greenhouse docs](https://docs.greenhouse.io/job-board.html)). That makes it the sanctioned source for true posting age and life. The Kaggle "LinkedIn Job Postings 2023–2024" dataset (CC BY-SA) has `applies`, `views`, `original_listed_time` and `closed_time` fields ([OpenML d/45952](https://api.openml.org/d/45952)). It is mostly US data, and as far as could be confirmed it is a cross-section rather than per-posting curves.

The legal constraint is mainly contractual. LinkedIn's User Agreement §8.2 bans scraping and bots, and Irish law governs it for EU users ([LinkedIn User Agreement](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/user-agreement)). The CJEU's *Ryanair* ruling, referred by the Dutch Hoge Raad, lets owners of unprotected databases enforce such terms by contract (C-30/14, [Pinsent Masons](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/news/website-operators-can-prohibit-screen-scraping-of-unprotected-data-via-terms-and-conditions-says-eu-court-in-ryanair-case)). *Innoweb v Wegener* found that systematically re-querying a job-ad-like database can infringe the sui generis database right (C-202/12, [IPKat](https://ipkitten.blogspot.com/2013/12/stepping-on-gaspedaal-cjeu-rules-on-re.html)). In the US, hiQ ended its dispute with a **$500,000 stipulated judgment** and agreed to delete its data ([Proskauer](https://newmedialaw.proskauer.com/2022/12/08/hiq-and-linkedin-reach-proposed-settlement-in-landmark-scraping-case/)). The GDPR exposure can be designed out. The Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens holds that private scraping of personal data is "almost never" lawful ([IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/netherlands-dpa-issues-guidance-against-web-scraping)), so a panel should store only job ID, company, title, location, timestamps and the count, with no recruiter names or hiring-team blocks, and should honour robots.txt as CNIL guidance implies ([Clifford Chance](https://www.cliffordchance.com/insights/resources/blogs/talking-tech/en/articles/2025/06/web-scraping-for-ai-development--the-cnil-builds-on-edpb-guidanc.html)). For a small app, the realistic consequences are IP blocking and a cease-and-desist letter, not fines. Those consequences still make a scraped panel a deliberate, time-limited choice, not a default pipeline.

## Recommended projection for odds

The formula separates three things the current model merges: channel coverage, time-to-final growth, and how many later arrivals actually compete.

```
t      = max(1, days since FIRST seen / ATS first_published)   # never the repost date
F(t)   = min(1, (t / L)^a)
ahead  = C_t                                                  # applicants already in
later  = C_t * (1/F(t) - 1)                                   # projected later arrivals
N_comp = m_channel * (ahead + w * later)                      # competitors for this applicant
N_total= m_channel * C_t / F(t)                               # eventual pool (display only)
odds_interview ≈ P(still reviewing at t) * slots / N_comp     # then the app's fit adjustments
```

| Parameter | Default | Range | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| a (exponent) | 0.45 | 0.35 high-volume to 0.55 low-volume | Dice fit 0.40; Ashby 0.5–0.65; quintile split |
| L, rolling / no deadline | 14 days | 10–21 | ATS medians 8–14; Dice mean 9.4; LinkedIn "~3 weeks" |
| L, stated deadline | deadline − first seen | n/a | windowed NL postings |
| w, batch (deadline; public, academic, windowed traineeships) | 1.0 | n/a | review after close |
| w, rolling (private, no deadline) | 0.4 | 0.25–0.6 | judgement; direction supported, size unmeasured |
| m_channel, Easy Apply | 2.8 | 2.0–4.0 | 1/s, s ≈ 0.35 |
| m_channel, off-site apply | 1.9 | 1.0–3.0 | c/s, c ≈ 0.65 |
| m_channel, Dutch-language or hourly role | ×1.5 on the above | n/a | Indeed-dominant, s ≈ 0.2 |
| "Over 100/200" | C_t = cap + 1; lower bound only | widen upper band ×2 | display cap |
| No read date | impute t = 9 | n/a | app median; F(9) ≈ 0.82 |
| Time-factor cap | 1/F ≤ 3.5 (follows from t ≥ 1) | n/a | replaces the 6× total cap |
| Total multiplier cap | 8× | n/a | Easy Apply × early batch read |
| slots per opening | 6 (internship), 5 (other) | 4–10 | ~4 interviewees per hire; 3–5% interview rate |

Applied to the example of "48 applicants" on an off-site posting, the models diverge most at the ends of a posting's life. On day 2, the current model gives 180. The recommended model gives 142 under rolling review and 219 under batch. On day 9 the three are close: 84 currently, against 99 (rolling) and 111 (batch). On day 21 the current model shrinks to 55, while the recommended model holds at 91, because a late read still sees only LinkedIn's share of the pool. All of these sit inside the UK internship median-to-mean band of 63–101 or modestly above it, which is a useful sanity check.

Seven changes to the current model follow. Replace L = 28 with 14, or with the real deadline, because 28 measures LinkedIn's expiry. Lower a to 0.45 and let it fall to about 0.35 for postings whose count-per-day sits in the app's top quintile. Scrape the apply type (Easy Apply or off-site) and add the channel multiplier, which is the largest missing factor for any read after day 7. Split competitors into "ahead" and "later" and weight later arrivals by review mode, inferred from a stated closing date, the employer type and the source ATS. Stop treating counts with no read date as final; impute t = 9, which adds about 9–22% before the channel multiplier. Model "still reviewing" as a separate survival probability rather than inflating the pool for late readers. Redefine slots explicitly as hiring-manager or first-round interviews per opening, defaulting to 5–6, and multiply by the number of openings when the posting states it. Show N_comp as a range using the parameter bands, since every input except the display cap is an assumption.

The calibration plan runs from free and lawful to costly and contractually risky. First, at no cost, re-derive L from the app's own 506 closed postings, split by source. Wherever the employer uses Greenhouse, Lever or Ashby, pull `first_published` and the closure date from the public ATS board, which gives the employer-side life and a repost-proof age. Second, log every count the app already reads with its timestamp and the posting's first-seen date. Each user view becomes a panel observation. Fit a hierarchical power law by role bucket on these logs, treating "Over 100" as left-censored and closures as right-censored rather than dropping them. Third, add in-app outcome reporting (applied on day X, heard back or not, interview yes or no), with consent and a privacy notice. This is the only lawful source for slots, w and the "still reviewing" curve. Fourth, ask Dutch career services and graduate recruiters for "applications by source" breakdowns to pin down s and c, the least-measured inputs. Fifth, and only after explicitly accepting the LinkedIn ToS risk, run the 300-posting LinkedIn pilot at about $4–17, storing no personal data, then decide on a four-to-six-week, roughly 2,500-posting panel at $30–121 a month. The Kaggle cross-section can serve as a free US sanity check on the exponent at any point.

## Conclusion

The weakness in odds' projection was never the curve's shape. It was what the clock and the counter actually measure. A 28-day life looks like data but measures a platform timer. A LinkedIn count looks like the pool but measures one channel, and sometimes only clicks. Correcting both moves projections in opposite directions depending on read age, which is why the current model often looks plausible at the median read of nine days while being systematically wrong for early and late reads. Those early and late reads are where users most need guidance.

Modelling competition, rather than just counting applicants, is the more consequential shift. Under rolling review an applicant's odds depend on who is ahead and whether the employer is still reading, not on how many people eventually click Apply. The app's most valuable data asset is therefore its own users' application dates and outcomes. They calibrate the rolling weight, the slots and the survival curve, none of which any public source measures, and they carry none of the legal exposure of scraping.
